## Page 3: Wholesale locational signal (node level)

Each dot is one of the 1,022 IESO pricing nodes that have coordinates as sourced from openstreetmap and manual linking where possible which leads to 1,054 nodes have been linked and 32 having mismatched coordinates and cannot be drawn. Nodes are coloured by their 35-day mean of day-ahead locational marginal price, congestion or loss, from August 30 to October 3, 2026.

**This is a wholesale signal, not what customers pay.** Nodal prices are a signal for generators and dispatchable resources. Retail customers served by distributors settle at the Ontario zonal price. The page therefore shows how the energy value of an injection varies by location. It does not show a retail price or a distribution deferral value.

Locational variation in the energy price is small in this window. The 35-day mean LMP across nodes was $49.15/MWh with a standard deviation of $1.85, and 96 percent of nodes fell between $43.70 and $52.55. Congestion is close to zero for most nodes: the median is $0.009/MWh, 42.3 percent of nodes are within one cent of zero, and only 7.7 percent exceed one dollar. The largest positive value is $1.62. Losses explain nearly all the spread. The correlation between LMP and its loss component is 0.95, so the LMP map is largely a map of losses.

The ten most positive congestion readings are all identical, at $1.615583/MWh, and all are northwestern load nodes (Kenora, Dryden, Burleigh, Cat Lake and Crow River among them). That identical value is what a single binding constraint produces, and it is not a data error. The most negative values are northwestern aggregate and generator nodes, which are excluded from the distributor-level signals on the next page.

**Reading this against the OEB's proposed tariff.** The OEB describes a net billing tariff with several parts: a dynamic energy price (locational marginal pricing is its example), a generation capacity price tied to the IESO capacity auction, and transmission and distribution capacity prices that vary by time and location and are zero where no constraint exists. This page covers only the first. In this window the energy component varies by location by a few dollars, while the hourly energy value on Page 1 varied by hundreds of dollars over time. These are different measures (a spread of 35-day means across nodes, against hourly values over a longer window), so I treat the comparison as an indication only. It suggests that most of the value a time- and location-based tariff can reflect would come from timing and from the capacity components, which LMP does not capture, and less from the locational energy price in a shoulder-season period like this one.

Node-to-zone coverage is 54.2 percent (572 of 1,054 nodes). It is below the 90 percent standard I set for aggregation, because the station table used for zoning does not include many generator and intertie nodes and the IESO node-zone map is not public. I therefore show no zone-level results. Four nodes with suspect coordinates are excluded, and I have not altered any coordinates. A window of about five weeks cannot speak to winter or summer congestion, and a near-zero reading now does not imply a near-zero annual reading.
