Ontario DER Monitor

The OEB's Report to the Minister on the Valuation of Distributed Energy Resources (docket EB-2025-0268) recommends that net metering be replaced by net billing, with injected electricity paid through a time and location-based tariff. The Ministry of Energy and Mines has not made that change. These four pages put numbers on the argument using free public IESO and OEB data. They test one slice of it, the wholesale energy component, and they do not cover the capacity components of the tariff the OEB describes. Every figure is a sample from a defined window, and each page states its window. Static dashboard; all figures computed from the processed files below.

Commentary

Net metering value wedge

For each of 1,848 summer hours (July 1 to mid-September 2026, about 77 days), I compared two prices; the first is the wholesale energy value of electricity, measured as the day-ahead Ontario zonal price plus the LFDA uplift. The second is what a net-metered customer on the regulated price plan is credited for each injected kilowatt-hour, which is the commodity rate for that hour's time-of-use period. The difference is the wedge.

On the time-of-use plan the wedge averaged $59.52/MWh, against a mean wholesale energy value of $66.17/MWh. Those two figures imply an average retail credit of about 12.6 cents per kWh or 125.69/MWh, so roughly 47 percent of the credit in this window was not backed by wholesale energy value. On the ultra-low overnight plan the average wedge was $71.65/MWh.

The retail credit moves in three fixed steps (9.8, 15.7 and 20.3 cents per kWh on time-of-use) that are set a year ahead. Wholesale value in the same window ranged from $23.27/MWh to $500.13/MWh, with a median of $50.84. Three patterns follow from that.

  • The time-of-use wedge was largest at on-peak ($117.15/MWh), then mid-peak ($80.21) and off-peak ($39.56). Retail steps track the daily shape of demand, but not its scale.
  • On the ultra-low overnight plan, the overnight period averaged -$1.57/MWh. Retail and wholesale were at parity there. The evening on-peak period (4 to 9 p.m.) averaged $276.57/MWh, the largest wedge of any period. That period falls after solar output has faded, so it is more relevant to storage than to rooftop solar.
  • In 149 of 1,848 hours (8.1 percent) the time-of-use wedge was negative: the wholesale price exceeded the retail credit. The minimum, -$391.61/MWh, came in a single hour when the wholesale price reached $500. A fixed credit therefore under-signals scarcity as well as over-signalling abundance.

The OEB found that crediting injected electricity at the retail rate gives no adequate price signal, because the retail rate also recovers capacity reserve and the cost of conservation, storage and industry programs. It also found that the regulated price plans are built for simplicity and predictability, which makes them a poor measure of what injected power is worth at a given hour. The wedge puts a number on that gap for the energy component. The OEB's own framing is careful, and I follow it. Residential distribution rates are fixed and do not depend on volume, so net-metered residential customers still pay for the distribution assets they use. Because the OEB treats the cost imbalance as currently small and expected to grow with adoption, this page's default headline is commodity-only. It is a conservative lower bound and leaves out delivery charges.

This doesn't does not mean that net-metered customers are overpaid by $59.52/MWh. The regulated price plan prices include an estimate of the Global Adjustment, which recovers real costs of contracted supply. The wedge is a measure of credit beyond wholesale energy value, not a measure of cost shifted to other customers. Three further limits apply.

  • The solar shape used elsewhere in the pipeline is province-wide transmission-visible solar, not rooftop output, and the figures above are all-hours averages, not weighted by solar injection. Midday periods carry different wedges from the all-hours average, so a solar-weighted wedge will differ.
  • The window is summer only. A full twelve-month window of the new market price will not close until about July 2027, and I will not publish an annual figure before then.
  • Tariff coverage ends October 31, 2026. The new regulated price plan prices take effect November 1, and I will add them when the OEB announces them.

Commentary

Page 2: DER adoption baseline

Net-metered capacity per 1,000 customers for each local distribution company, from the OEB's Reporting and Record-keeping Requirements (data year 2024, the newest vintage published). The map covers 52 distributors on the colour scale. Hydro One Networks is grey because its territory spans several electrical zones and a single per-customer figure would mislead. Five distributors have no data in the OEB files: Attawapiskat, Fort Albany, Kashechewan, Hydro One Remote Communities and Cornwall Street Railway Light and Power. They are drawn grey and are never shown as zero.

Across reported distributors, net metering totals 104.3 MW for 4,896 customers, about 0.12 percent of the 4.14 million customers in the reporting set. The per-distributor median is 0.31 MW per 1,000 customers and the 90th percentile is 1.57. The highest values are Hearst Power (3.94), Bluewater (2.34), Essex (1.95), PUC Distribution (1.85) and Renfrew (1.68). The lowest are Rideau St. Lawrence (0.022), Northern Ontario Wires (0.023, no net-metered customers) and Hydro Hawkesbury (0.024, none). Hearst's top ranking rests on six customers in a base of 2,680 and should be read as a small-number effect.

The average installation is about 21 kW (104.3 MW over 4,896 customers). That is larger than a typical home system, which suggests the stock includes commercial and agricultural installations as well as homes. I have not confirmed the split by customer class.

The OEB's report says net metering adoption is low, at under one percent, and that six distributors exceeded the one percent threshold in 2024. Its own projection, extrapolating 2019 to 2024 reporting, is twenty distributors above the threshold by 2030 and about thirty, half of all distributors, by 2037. The two measures are not the same. The OEB's threshold is net-metered generation as a share of a distributor's three-year average annual peak load. This map uses capacity per 1,000 customers. The 0.12 percent figure above is a share of customers, so it does not test the OEB's one percent test. A peak-load denominator would, and adding one is the logical next step for this page.

Net metering (Table 1) and embedded generation by fuel (Table 3) are separate layers and are never added together. Net metering is the mechanism the OEB proposes to replace. Table 3 resources are mostly contracted or procured under other frameworks. In Table 3 solar dominates at 800 MW across 51 distributors, followed by fossil fuel at 406 MW across 16, larger than all non-solar renewables combined. Storage, both exporting and non-exporting, is 109 MW across 12 distributors. This is useful context for the OEB's distinction between behind-the-meter resources and larger resources that use other compensation pathways.

Limits. The data are for 2024. Service-area boundaries are indicative and were not validated for legal precision. Orillia is grouped with Hydro One Networks to reflect its integration.

Commentary

Page 3: Wholesale locational signal (node level)

Each dot is one of the 1,022 IESO pricing nodes that have coordinates as sourced from openstreetmap and manual linking where possible which leads to 1,054 nodes have been linked and 32 having mismatched coordinates and cannot be drawn. Nodes are coloured by their 35-day mean of day-ahead locational marginal price, congestion or loss, from August 30 to October 3, 2026.

This is a wholesale signal, not what customers pay. Nodal prices are a signal for generators and dispatchable resources. Retail customers served by distributors settle at the Ontario zonal price. The page therefore shows how the energy value of an injection varies by location. It does not show a retail price or a distribution deferral value.

Locational variation in the energy price is small in this window. The 35-day mean LMP across nodes was $49.15/MWh with a standard deviation of $1.85, and 96 percent of nodes fell between $43.70 and $52.55. Congestion is close to zero for most nodes: the median is $0.009/MWh, 42.3 percent of nodes are within one cent of zero, and only 7.7 percent exceed one dollar. The largest positive value is $1.62. Losses explain nearly all the spread. The correlation between LMP and its loss component is 0.95, so the LMP map is largely a map of losses.

The ten most positive congestion readings are all identical, at $1.615583/MWh, and all are northwestern load nodes (Kenora, Dryden, Burleigh, Cat Lake and Crow River among them). That identical value is what a single binding constraint produces, and it is not a data error. The most negative values are northwestern aggregate and generator nodes, which are excluded from the distributor-level signals on the next page.

Reading this against the OEB's proposed tariff. The OEB describes a net billing tariff with several parts: a dynamic energy price (locational marginal pricing is its example), a generation capacity price tied to the IESO capacity auction, and transmission and distribution capacity prices that vary by time and location and are zero where no constraint exists. This page covers only the first. In this window the energy component varies by location by a few dollars, while the hourly energy value on Page 1 varied by hundreds of dollars over time. These are different measures (a spread of 35-day means across nodes, against hourly values over a longer window), so I treat the comparison as an indication only. It suggests that most of the value a time- and location-based tariff can reflect would come from timing and from the capacity components, which LMP does not capture, and less from the locational energy price in a shoulder-season period like this one.

Node-to-zone coverage is 54.2 percent (572 of 1,054 nodes). It is below the 90 percent standard I set for aggregation, because the station table used for zoning does not include many generator and intertie nodes and the IESO node-zone map is not public. I therefore show no zone-level results. Four nodes with suspect coordinates are excluded, and I have not altered any coordinates. A window of about five weeks cannot speak to winter or summer congestion, and a near-zero reading now does not imply a near-zero annual reading.

Commentary

Page 4: Where DER is growing and where wholesale value is

For each local distribution company, the horizontal axis is the average wholesale locational premium across its load nodes, defined as the difference between day-ahead LMP and the Ontario zonal price in matched hours (September 2 to October 3, 2026). The vertical axis is net-metered capacity per 1,000 customers from Page 2. The test covers 33 approved distributors. Distributors whose nodes are too few or too varied are excluded from the correlation and are drawn hollow.

The rank correlation is Spearman rho = -0.13 (p = 0.46, n = 33). That is no detectable relationship. The wholesale premium itself is small, averaging -$0.13/MWh with a range from -$6.03 to +$3.30. It tracks marginal losses almost entirely (correlation 0.96 with the loss component). Only 3 of the 33 distributors have a mean congestion value above $1 in absolute terms.

With 33 observations, a correlation of -0.13 is compatible with a true rank correlation from roughly -0.45 to +0.22 (an approximate 95 percent interval). So the result is an absence of evidence and not evidence of absence. Four further limits apply.

  • Eleven of the 33 distributors rest on a single node, including both extremes. Hearst (-$6.03, and the highest adoption) and Sioux Lookout (+$3.30, near the lowest adoption) are illustrations and not robust findings.
  • Adoption is a 2024 stock that built up over years, set against 2026 prices for about a month.
  • Roof stock, income, installer presence and local programs shape adoption and are not in the data.
  • Classifying nodes by name pattern moves results. Five distributors shifted by more than $0.50/MWh when I restricted the calculation to load nodes.

The OEB's review was a qualitative assessment against a value stack. It did not claim an empirical footprint, and this page does not supply one. What the page shows is narrower. Under a flat retail-rate credit, a customer has no financial reason to favour a location, and across the distributors tested, adoption does not line up with the one locational energy signal available in public data. That signal is small and mostly reflects losses, so even a customer paid at LMP would face little locational incentive in this window. If the OEB's tariff is implemented, I would expect any locational steering to come from the transmission and distribution capacity components, where constraints exist, and not from the energy price. The data to test that, such as regional planning needs and non-wires solution locations, is not in this dashboard yet.

This page will be rerun on a longer window as nodal data accumulates, and I will report any change in the sign or size of the correlation.

Methods and caveats

Definitions

  • OEMP (Ontario Energy Market Price) = DA-OZP + LFDA, the IESO's day-ahead Ontario zonal price plus the Hourly LFDA addend.
  • OZP (Ontario zonal price): since the May 2025 market renewal, the successor to HOEP as the headline Ontario price. Older data is labelled "HOEP era" and never spliced into OEMP.
  • LFDA: the IESO Hourly LFDA addend; the feed's value column is headed "LFDC Rate ($/MWh)".
  • RPP commodity offset: the effective-dated RPP TOU/ULO commodity rate overlaid against OEMP. The wedge is the difference between the two. RPP rates include a Global Adjustment estimate and RPP cost recovery, so the wedge is not only an energy-value gap.
